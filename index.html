Roots and Realities

My story is one of resilience and deep roots. I am an African American girl of Indigenous descent, born and raised in Indiana. I grew up in Haughville, a neighborhood that shaped my early life. At 15, I became pregnant and homeless, and by the age of 19, I was a mother of three. We lived in subsidized housing off Indiana Avenue until we were displaced to make room for student housing for IU students. My parents met as neighbors in a duplex on 30th and College, across from Frog’s Candy Store. My father grew up off Michigan Road, and my mother was raised in subsidized housing on the east side of Indianapolis. My paternal grandfather lived on Indiana Avenue until he was drafted into the war and forced to relocate upon his return. My grandmother, an Indigenous woman from rural Mississippi, met him while he worked on the railroad. These family histories and personal experiences form the foundation of my identity and the lens through which I see the world.

My Lens: What I See and What I Don’t

My identity as an African American and Indigenous woman shapes how I see and interpret the world. My experiences with displacement, motherhood, and community influence what I notice and what I may overlook. I acknowledge the biases and privileges that come with my perspective, and I strive to reflect critically on what is visible and invisible in my lived experiences.

Research Prompts

Prompt 1: Reflection on Research

I learned in Africana Studies that research is about investigating a question. My personal definition of research has always been: form a hypothesis, create methods, experiment or investigate, collect data, and form a conclusion. As a biologist, this is what research means to me. However, even though I am part of Black culture here in Indiana, I feel as if I don’t know enough about Indianapolis’ history within my community—beyond the birth of the Klan.

Prompt 2: Research Ethics

I use ethics every day—on campus and as a mom. The Black community is the reason research ethics exists because of the unethical treatment we have endured throughout history. Our experiences shaped the standards that protect participants today.

Prompt 3: Qualitative vs Quantitative

Qualitative research focuses on understanding experiences, meanings, and perspectives through interviews, observations, and narratives. Quantitative research emphasizes numbers, measurements, and statistical analysis to identify patterns and test hypotheses. Both methods are essential for building a complete picture of reality.

Prompt 4: Literature Review

A literature review synthesizes existing research to identify gaps, trends, and key findings. It helps frame your research question and ensures your work builds on prior knowledge.

Prompt 5: Research Design

Research design is the blueprint for your study. It organizes methods, timelines, and resources. Being organized ensures validity and reliability, making your findings credible.

Prompt 6: Paradigms

Paradigms shape how we view reality and conduct research. Understanding where the Black community fits within these frameworks is essential for inclusive and ethical scholarship.

Prompt 7: Measurement & Sampling in Qualitative Research

  1. Measurement in qualitative research: focuses on depth and meaning rather than numbers. Researchers use coding, themes, and patterns to interpret data and ensure rich context.
  2. Validity & reliability in social science: use strategies like triangulation, member checking, and clear documentation to maintain credibility and trustworthiness.
  3. Effective sampling: purposeful (purposive) sampling is often most effective because it selects participants who can provide rich, relevant insights for the research question.
  4. Additional thoughts: measurement can feel subjective, but transparency and rigor help reduce bias.

Prompt 8: Abstracts vs Introductions

  1. Difference: an abstract is a concise summary of the entire study (purpose, methods, results, conclusions). An introduction provides background, context, and the research question, setting the stage for the study.
  2. Significance: the abstract helps readers quickly understand the scope and findings, while the introduction builds interest and explains why the study matters.
  3. What I learned: using Swales & Feak’s abstract moves, I can construct an effective abstract, define what an abstract is, and distinguish between different types.

What I Carry Forward

As I move forward, I carry the lessons and strength from my past. My story is a testament to perseverance and identity. I hope to inspire others who face similar challenges and encourage reflection on what we see and what remains unseen.
